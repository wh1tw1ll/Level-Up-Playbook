## SLIDE 16  |  YOUR TURN — HOW WE MAINTAIN URGENCY

**Lead: Whitney**   *Topics 2 and 8  |  About 3-4 minutes*

**OPEN**

Urgency has to be built into the structure of the project — or it doesn't hold. You can't depend on any one person having a good week. The system has to produce urgency on its own.

Here's what that looks like.

**MEETING CADENCE**

Every Thursday, your leadership team gets a 30-minute executive update from us. Tight, prepared, ends with decisions. No "we'll circle back."

During construction, OAC runs bi-weekly: owner's rep, CMAR, key A/E leads. Monthly, we expand to include owner leadership — one consistent format, one source of truth.

Every morning on site, 15-minute standing huddle. My team, the CMAR superintendent, key trade foremen. Yesterday's results, today's critical path, what's blocked. That huddle is where we catch problems before they hit the OAC meeting.

**ACTION ITEM DISCIPLINE**

Every meeting produces action items. Owner. Date. One source of truth.

I review the aging report every week. Anything past seven days gets my personal attention. Anything past fourteen gets formally escalated. Not because I'm a micromanager — because open items compound.

**DECISION DEADLINES**

Every open decision gets a due date. Owner decisions: five business days. Design decisions: ten. Emergencies: 24 hours. The system is built to protect the Chiefs' time.

When I bring a decision to you, it comes with three options — low, medium, high cost — each with schedule and risk impact laid out. You're reviewing a recommendation, not an open question. If I can't narrow it to three options with a clear recommendation, I'm not done with my job yet.

**ESCALATION**

When something material comes up, the path is fast but not reactive. Identify it, analyze it, build the recommendation, get it in front of you within 24 to 48 hours. The difference between crisis management and controlled urgency is that gap — and we close it fast.

## SLIDE 22  |  YOUR TURN — CHANGE MANAGEMENT PROCESS

**Lead: Whitney / Gary**   *Topics 4 and 8  |  Whitney leads, Gary supports*

**SET THE FRAMEWORK**

Change management is where the owner either gets protected or gets hurt. The difference is whether you have a process in place before the first change event hits — not after.

Here's our process. Six steps. I'll walk them fast.

**THE SIX STEPS**

One: foundation. A plan and process in place before Change Order Number One exists. The CMAR knows the rules before they submit anything. That's non-negotiable.

Two: pre-bulletin coordination. Before anything gets formally submitted, we ask: why is this change necessary, what alternatives exist, what are the cost, schedule, and operational impacts. That conversation happens with the architect and engineers before pricing — not after.

Three: the bulletin page flip. Detailed review with the architect, engineers, and CM. Align scope, validate intent, look for mitigation opportunities — before we ever see a number.

Four: the proposed change order itself. Complete backup. Quantity validation. Schedule impact. Contractual justification. Not a summary — the full package. If they can't document it, we don't evaluate it.

Five: detailed review against the GMP. The same team that negotiated the GMP evaluates every proposed change against the assumptions, scope coverage, and contract structure we set up. That continuity matters — you don't want a different set of eyes on the change than the ones that negotiated the baseline.

Six: the owner recap and recommendation. Clear executive summary — history of the issue, options evaluated, cost and schedule impacts, recommended path forward. You make the decision with full information.

**WHY IT WORKS**

By the time a change order reaches you, we've already done the work. You're not reacting — you're approving something we've already analyzed and challenged.

**SLIDE 32 — COMMISSIONING, SYSTEMS INTEGRATION, TURNOVER**

**Start at procurement**

Most teams treat commissioning as a closeout activity. By the time you get there, it's too late to do it right.

We set testing requirements when we procure the system. The vendor knows from day one what they'll be tested on. That changes how they design, install, and document their work.

Result: zero Day-One operational failures at Freedom Park. Commissioning plan developed during design, executed during construction, closed before occupancy.

**Systems integration**

Systems have to be tested together, not just individually. AV and IT and broadcast. Security and access control. HVAC and building management. Every combination gets a defined test protocol and acceptance criteria. If it doesn't pass, we don't sign off on it.

**Turnover**

O&Ms delivered before staff training starts. Training happens during commissioning — not after you take the keys. Warranties organized and indexed. As-builts verified against field conditions. Punch list managed to zero before occupancy.

The turnover package gets built throughout the project. By substantial completion, documentation is 90% done.
